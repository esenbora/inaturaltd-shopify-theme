What is animal testing?

Animal testing means experiments carried out on living animals. Rabbits, rodents, dogs and fish are exposed to synthetic substances to assess whether they might be harmful to human health. It happens for new medicines, but also for make-up, shampoo, cleaning products and laundry detergent. It's a practice with a long history — the first documented animal experiment dates back to 1628.

Why animal testing is no longer necessary

Animal testing is increasingly controversial, and more and more shoppers are choosing cruelty-free products instead. The animals are often subjected to stressful conditions and painful experiments.

There's also a real question over whether it even works. A human body doesn't tolerate the same substances in the same way a rodent does, so the results aren't always reliable in the first place.

Modern alternatives to animal testing

Thankfully, we now have excellent alternatives. In-vitro testing and advanced computer models routinely deliver more accurate, more relevant results — without any animal suffering.

How to recognise cruelty-free skincare

Spotting genuinely cruelty-free products can be tricky, but there are a few reliable ways to check as a shopper:

  • Look for recognised certifications. The Leaping Bunny logo — the international gold standard in the UK and Europe — is awarded only to brands that meet strict cruelty-free criteria.
  • Check for the PETA cruelty-free logo, which is increasingly used to signal that a product hasn't been tested on animals.
  • Remember: cruelty-free is not the same as vegan. A product can be cruelty-free yet still contain animal-derived ingredients. If you want to be certain a product is vegan, always check the ingredients list too.

Watch out for greenwashing and marketing tricks

In the UK and EU there's no legal requirement to state on the packaging whether a product is cruelty-free. Many responsible brands choose to declare it voluntarily for transparency — but some use misleading claims like "not tested on animals" or "animal-friendly" that paint a rosier picture than reality. The finished product may not have been tested on animals, while the individual ingredients were tested separately.
